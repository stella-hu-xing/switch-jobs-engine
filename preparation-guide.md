1. Coding and data structures fundamentals
Staff-level loops still usually include 1-2 coding rounds, though less pure-algorithm and more systems-flavored (e.g. rate limiters, LRU caches, concurrency primitives, log/metric aggregation logic). Cover: data structures (arrays, hash maps, trees, graphs, heaps), algorithmic patterns (two pointers, sliding window, BFS/DFS, dynamic programming basics), and time/space complexity analysis. Practice writing clean, correct code under time pressure in whatever language the company uses (Go, Python, Java are common at these companies).

2. System design and distributed systems at scale
This is usually the heaviest-weighted round at staff level. Cover: distributed systems fundamentals (CAP theorem, consistency models, consensus algorithms like Raft/Paxos at a conceptual level, replication and partitioning strategies), designing for scale (load balancing, caching layers, message queues/Kafka, sharding), reliability patterns (circuit breakers, retries with backoff, graceful degradation, failure domains), and how to structure a 45-60 minute design conversation (clarify requirements, define scope, propose high-level design, dive into a component, discuss trade-offs and bottlenecks).

3. Observability and telemetry deep dive
Cover the full breadth even where you have depth already, to be sure nothing is assumed: metrics vs logs vs traces and when to use each, OpenTelemetry's architecture (collectors, exporters, instrumentation, context propagation), cardinality and its cost implications at scale, SLIs/SLOs/error budgets and how they drive engineering decisions, incident management and on-call practices, and designing an observability platform from scratch (ingestion, storage, query layer, alerting) as a standalone system-design topic.

4. Kubernetes, AWS, and infrastructure-as-code
Cover: core Kubernetes concepts (pods, deployments, services, ingress, StatefulSets, operators/CRDs), cluster networking and service mesh basics (Istio/Linkerd concepts), AWS core services relevant to platform work (EC2, EKS, VPC, IAM, S3, Lambda, autoscaling groups), infrastructure-as-code (Terraform patterns, state management), and CI/CD pipeline design (build/test/deploy stages, canary and blue-green deployments, rollback strategies).

5. AI/ML infrastructure fundamentals (the genuinely new material)
This is the domain-specific gap-filler for the AI-infra pivot specifically - treat as new material to learn, not review. Cover: how model serving works (batching, latency vs throughput trade-offs, frameworks like vLLM/TensorRT/Triton at a conceptual level), GPU scheduling and orchestration basics (why GPUs are harder to schedule than CPUs, fractional GPU allocation, node pools), vector databases and embedding storage at a high level, LLM-specific observability concepts (token usage tracking, prompt/response tracing, cost-per-request monitoring, hallucination/quality monitoring), and basic MLOps concepts (model versioning, feature stores, training vs inference infrastructure separation).

6. Staff-level behavioral and technical-leadership stories
Staff-level loops always include a round assessing scope of influence beyond your own code. Prepare stories (using the structures covered earlier - lead with the outcome, then reasoning) covering: a time you influenced technical direction across multiple teams without direct authority, a time you mentored or leveled up other engineers, a time you disagreed with a decision and how you handled it, a time you had to make a build-vs-buy or platform-investment trade-off call, and a time a project failed or underperformed and what you learned. Staff interviews probe for judgment and organizational impact, not just individual technical skill.

7. Company- and team-specific technical research
For each target company (Atlassian, Xero, ClickHouse, Grafana, etc.), research: their actual product architecture as far as it's publicly documented (engineering blogs, conference talks), what specific team you're interviewing for and its current priorities, recent technical challenges they've talked about publicly, and how their AI investments specifically relate to the team you're targeting. This lets you ask sharp questions and tailor system-design answers to problems they actually have.

8. Mock interviews with structured feedback
Run at least 2-3 full mock interviews (system design and behavioral, ideally with another engineer or a structured tool) before real interviews. Time yourself on the coding round. Get specific feedback rather than general impressions - ask reviewers to flag any moment where your reasoning wasn't clear or where you missed an obvious follow-up question. Treat each mock as a chance to tighten structure, not just confirm you already know the material.
